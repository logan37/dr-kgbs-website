# Rules on what an Indian (Telangana) registered medical practitioner may publish on a practice website and Google Business Profile (as of Nov 2026)

> Research notes, not legal advice. Researched 16 Nov 2026. The most important finding: on **6 Oct 2026 the NMC issued "Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication by Hospitals/Medical Institutions and Registered Medical Practitioners"** (No. R-13014/01/2024-Ethics), effective immediately. These guidelines sit on top of the IMC 2002 Regulations and are now the most specific rulebook for doctors' websites and social media. ## Which professional-conduct rules apply in 2026: the 2002 Regulations, the 2023 Regulations, or something newer?

### Takeaway
In Nov 2026 the governing code is still the **Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002**. The NMC adopted it in Aug 2023 when it put its own 2023 RMP Conduct Regulations "in abeyance", and no source found says the 2023 Regulations have been revived. The **NMC Ethical Advertising Guidelines of 6 Oct 2026** apply on top of the 2002 Regulations. They are expressly "read with" those Regulations, and they are what a State Medical Council (SMC) will now apply to websites and social media.

### Cited Findings
- In Aug 2023 the NMC placed the Registered Medical Practitioner (Professional Conduct) Regulations, 2023 in abeyance until a further gazette notification. It adopted the IMC 2002 Regulations "with immediate effect" as if made under the NMC Act, 2019. — [Legality Simplified](https://legalitysimplified.com/?p=36875)
- The government put the 2023 regulations on hold after a representation by the Indian Medical Association (IMA). — [The Wire](https://thewire.in/health/nmc-puts-new-guidelines-on-doctors-ethics-in-abeyance-after-imas-meeting-with-health-minister)

### Inferences
- For a private pulmonologist in Telangana, the binding standards are the IMC 2002 Regulations (Chapters 1, 6 and 7) plus the Oct 2026 NMC Guidelines. Neither strictly permits nor forbids a personal practice website as such. Both turn on whether the content is "promotional"/"solicitation" or factual "patient information".
- The Guidelines are a public notice, not a gazetted regulation. A court might later test whether the graded penalties (fines, fixed suspension periods) are enforceable. Until then, SMCs are likely to treat them as the operative standard of "professional misconduct".

### Gaps
- No source found on any court challenge to, or stay of, the Oct 2026 Guidelines, or on any IMA reaction to them.
- The official NMC gazette and website could not be checked directly. The nmc.org.in 2002-Regulations page returned 404, so the 2002 text came from a bare-act mirror.
- Whether the NMC plans a fresh RMP Conduct Regulations notification for 2026–27 is unknown.

## Specific rules: self-promotion, testimonials and reviews, superlatives and success rates, fees, photos, qualifications and registration number, websites and social media, signboards, AI, telemedicine

### Takeaway
The rules allow **factual, verifiable, non-promotional patient information**: name, recognised qualifications, registration number, specialty, services, address, timings and declared fees. They prohibit **self-promotion and solicitation**, which covers testimonials, requesting or sharing patient reviews on social media, "best/No.1/100% success" claims, success rates, number of patients treated, before/after images, celebrity patients, discounts or offers, fake or manipulated reviews, AI-generated promotional campaigns and promotional use of the doctor's photograph. Electronic-media posts must carry the doctor's name, qualifications and SMC/NMR registration number.

### Cited Findings
**Definition and scope (Oct 2026 NMC Guidelines)** — all from the [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- 3.2 "Advertisement" means any communication "which directly or indirectly solicits, promotes or is intended to promote the professional services, reputation, skills, qualifications, achievements, facilities or commercial healthcare services of an RMP". "digital, social-media, sponsored, targeted, influencer-led, search-engine, platform-based, audio-visual, AI-generated or AI-assisted promotional communications shall be treated as advertising where their content or manner of dissemination has a promotional character."
- 3.2 Explanation I exempts information shared "in the normal course of discharge of information to the public at large, without same being promotional and without any intent for commercial benefit".
- 3.2 Explanation II: information about equipment, technology, diagnostic facilities or services "may be disseminated as factual information" if it has no "claims of superiority, guaranteed accuracy, guaranteed outcomes, comparative claims or other misleading or unverifiable representations." This is relevant to listing e.g. spirometry/PFT, bronchoscopy or sleep study facilities.
- **3.2 Explanation III (mandatory disclosure): "RMP while publishing any Electronic media posts should transparently disclose their name, qualifications, registration status and SMR/NMR Registration number."**
- **3.2 Explanation V (reviews): "No RMP … shall solicit, purchase, procure, manipulate or cause to be published fake, paid or misleading patient reviews, ratings, testimonials, endorsements or recommendations for the promotion of medical services. An RMP shall not request or share patient testimonials, recommendations, endorsements or reviews for professional promotion on social media."**
- 3.5 "Medium of advertisement" expressly includes "Social media platforms, websites and blogs, messaging platforms (WhatsApp, Telegrams, Facebook etc.), Podcasts, Influencer marketing, Digital marketing campaigns, sponsored posts, artificial intelligence generated promotion … online healthcare platforms".
- 4.4: publishing through an agency, influencer or platform does not "absolve an RMP … from responsibility for content which it has authorized, commissioned, sponsored, adopted or knowingly permitted."

**Prohibited for RMPs (clause 8.1)** — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- (i) No advertising that creates "unnecessary demand for medical procedures, promote unnecessary diagnostics and promote fear-based marketing."
- (ii) "Shall not solicit patients directly or indirectly, promote his/her professional services through self-promotion, engage any third party for marketing medical services, permit the use of their name, image, voice, testimonial or endorsement for promotion of services or any products".
- (iii) "Shall not advertise either personal achievements or professional success rates or number of patients treated with illustrative assertions such as 'Guaranteed cures', 'Best doctors', 'No.1 Specialist', '100% success', 'painless treatment', 'Miracle treatment', 'Exclusive cure' or any other unverifiable or exaggerated claims".
- (iv) No endorsement of any drug, device, diagnostic product or health product using the doctor's name, photo, voice or professional status, "whether or not monetary or other consideration is received."
- (v) "Shall not publish cases for promotional purposes, advertise surgical results, display 'before and after' photographs, publicize celebrity patients, promote personal success stories unless published strictly for scientific or educational purposes with anonymized patient consent."
- (vi) No statements that create unrealistic expectations, misrepresent outcomes, conceal risks or promote unproven therapies or secret remedies.
- (vii)–(viii) No commission, referral fee or lead-generation fee. Agency or platform payments may not be linked to procuring individual patients. Pay-per-lead marketing arrangements are therefore risky.
- (ix) No "best", "No.1", "leading", "most trusted", "top", "number one", "unmatched" claims "unless the claim is objectively verifiable, based on a transparent and independently ascertainable methodology". Any award or ranking cited must be independently verifiable, and its methodology, date and awarding body must be disclosed.
- (x) No "discounts, limited-period offers, contests, coupons, gifts, cashbacks, referral benefits, free procedures" likely to encourage unnecessary care. "Any lawful disclosure of charges, packages or fees shall be factual, transparent and not misleading."
- (xi) No celebrity, influencer, patient, employee or third-party testimonials. No use of an intermediary "to do indirectly what the RMP … is prohibited from doing directly."
- (xii) No fake followers, likes, reviews, ratings or comments, and no manipulating "search rankings, visibility or algorithms to create a misleading impression of professional standing."

**Patient privacy (clauses 6, 7.4, 7.6)** — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- 6.2 "Patient consent shall not, by itself, make permissible any testimonial, endorsement, promotional patient story, before-and-after depiction, success claim … otherwise prohibited".
- 6.4 and 7.4: patients must not be publicly identifiable. Faces, names and identifying marks must be cropped, blurred or blacked out.
- 7.6: advertising that processes personal data must comply with the IT Act 2000 and the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and its 2025 Rules**. This is relevant to appointment and contact forms.

**AI (clause 7.2)** — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- "AI Generated promotional campaign for furtherance of commercial interest is prohibited." Compliant AI-generated content "shall compulsorily carry the source mark, explicitly stating that the origin of the content is AI."
- AI may not create or manipulate a patient's image, testimonial, voice or outcome, or synthetic endorsements. Disclosure is required where the AI nature is material.

**Photographs (clause 9.2)** — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- "Use of the photograph/image of an RMP shall not be permitted; where such use constitutes self-advertisement, solicitation or promotional publicity." A self-photograph on a letterhead or signboard is governed by Regs 6.1.1/6.1.2 of 2002 "and regarded as unethical conduct."

**Permitted (clauses 5, 8.2, 9.1, 9.3)** — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- 8.2(ii) / 9.3: formal announcements that are "factual, non-promotional and limited in scope" about starting practice, change of practice type, change of address, temporary absence, resumption or succession of practice, and "Public declaration of charge" / "consultation fees".
- 8.2(i): research-based communication on case studies published in reputed medical journals.
- 8.2(iii): public-health lectures or talks on TV, radio and electronic media "in their own name and designation, without promoting their employer".
- 8.2(iv): health-education pamphlets for patients visiting the clinic, with no guarantee of cure, superiority claims or unsubstantiated claims.
- 8.2(v): greetings on national or medical days without advertising.
- 5.1: participation in health awareness and educational content is allowed "provided that such activities do not promote personal practice, do not solicit patients and are not monetized through promotional marketing."
- 9.1: a hospital website directory of doctors with "names, recognised qualifications, specialties, registration details and availability … that serves a patient-information function; shall be regarded materially distinct from a paid or promotional advertisement". This is the closest official signal that a factual doctor-profile page counts as patient information.
- 9.4: healthcare platforms hosting RMPs (e.g. listing/booking apps) must prohibit paid ranking of doctors.

**Penalties (clause 10)** — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- Graded SMC action: first violation, warning and mandatory ethics training. Second, censure and monetary penalty. Third, suspension of 3–6 months. Serious violation ("Misleading cure claims, inducement for patients, digital mass solicitation"), suspension of 6–12 months. Repeated violations, removal from the register for 1–3 years. A show-cause notice and a reasoned order are required. Appeals go to the EMRB within 60 days (NMC Act s.30(3)), then a second appeal under s.30(4).

**IMC 2002 Regulations (still operative)** — [bare act text, bnblegal](https://bnblegal.com/bareact/the-indian-medical-council-professional-conduct-etiquette-and-ethics-regulations-2002/); also [FOGSI copy](https://www.fogsi.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/pdf/code_ethics.pdf), [Delhi Medical Council PDF](https://www.delhimedicalcouncil.org/pdf/EthicsRegulations-2002.pdf)
- 1.4.1: "Every physician shall display the registration number accorded to him by the State Medical Council/Medical Council of India in his clinic and in all his prescriptions, certificates, money receipts given to his patients."
- 1.4.2: "Physicians shall display as suffix to their names only recognised medical degrees" or certificates/diplomas/memberships/honours that confer professional knowledge.
- 1.8: "A physician should announce his fees before rendering service and not after the operation or treatment is under way."
- 6.1: "A physician shall not make use of him/her (or his/her name) as subject of any form or manner of advertising or publicity through any mode…". 6.1.1 bars "Soliciting of patients directly or indirectly". 6.1.2 bars "Printing of self photograph, or any such material of publicity in the letter head or on the sign board".
- 7.13: "It is improper for a physician to use an unusually large sign board and write on it anything other than his name, qualifications obtained from a University or a statutory body, titles and name of his speciality, registration number including the name of the State Medical Council under which registered", plus consulting hours.
- 7.14: patient secrets must not be disclosed.
- Ch. 8: the appropriate Medical Council holds an enquiry on complaints, with penalties up to removal from the register, temporary or permanent. Decisions are due within 6 months.

**Telemedicine**
- The Oct 2026 Guidelines do not mention telemedicine. — [Medical Dialogues summary](https://medicaldialogues.in/health-news/nmc/nmc-issues-ethical-advertising-public-communication-guidelines-for-doctors-hospitals-details-180731)

### Inferences
- A personal website is not banned, but every element is judged on whether it is "promotional". The safest design treats the site as a digital signboard plus a patient-information leaflet: the 7.13 signboard items (name, university/statutory qualifications, specialty, registration number with the SMC name, consulting hours), fees declared factually, address, contact and appointment details, and neutral, evidence-based explanations of conditions and services.
- The doctor's photograph is a grey zone. The 2002 Regs 6.1.2 bar photos on letterheads and signboards. The 2026 clause 9.2 bars photos only "where such use constitutes self-advertisement". A modest, single professional headshot on an "About" page that serves identification is arguably patient information. Hero banners, promotional collages and ad creatives with the doctor's face are risky. A conservative approach is to skip the photo or use one small identification photo.
- Patient reviews: given Explanation V, the site should **not embed or quote patient reviews or testimonials**, should not run "rate us 5 stars" campaigns, and should not share reviews on social media. A plain factual link such as "Google Business Profile" is the lowest-risk way to let organic third-party reviews exist. Even this is untested; an ultra-conservative reading omits any review link.
- "Experience" statements ("15 years in practice") are factual. Counts of patients or procedures ("10,000+ patients treated") are expressly barred by 8.1(iii). Hospital affiliations and past positions are factual, but avoid promoting an employer (8.2(iii)).
- Words to avoid: best, top, leading, No.1, expert in curing, guaranteed, painless, permanent cure, miracle, advanced/most modern (if used comparatively), success rate, "trusted by thousands".
- Any AI-generated images or illustrations on the site should be labelled as AI-generated (7.2). Do not use AI-generated "patients" or stock "patient testimonial" visuals.

### Gaps
- No official NMC FAQ or clarification (clause 12 allows them) was found yet on whether a personal practice website or a doctor's headshot is acceptable.
- Telemedicine-specific advertising rules (e.g. the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines, 2020) were not researched in this pass. Whether they add website display requirements (registration number on teleconsult pages, etc.) is unverified.
- The exact amendment history of the 2002 Regulations (e.g. 2009/2016 amendments) was not checked.

## Google's own policies: Business Profile reviews and Google Ads healthcare (India)

### Takeaway
Google allows a business to **ask customers for genuine reviews**. It prohibits incentives, review gating (selectively asking happy patients), discouraging negative reviews, staff-quota or scripted requests, and conflict-of-interest or fake reviews. The NMC Guidelines are stricter than Google: a doctor must not request or share testimonials or reviews for promotion on social media. **NMC rules override Google's permission**, so a doctor should not actively solicit Google reviews. Google Ads restricts prescription-drug promotion, speculative or experimental treatments and online pharmacies in India. Ordinary clinic-service ads are not certification-gated, but any paid ad still counts as an "advertisement" under the NMC Guidelines.

### Cited Findings
- Google Maps user-contributed content policy: "Fake engagement is not allowed and will be removed". Merchants may not "Offer incentives – such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" for reviews, may not "Discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers", should not pressure customers on premises, and may not request specific content (including naming staff). Conflict-of-interest content is prohibited (employment, contractual or personal affiliations). Merchants may "Solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience" without incentives or influence. — [Google Maps contribution policy](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114)
- Google Business Profile guidelines allow merchants to post contact information in responses to reviews and Q&A. Promotions must link to terms. — [Google Business Profile guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177)
- Google Ads Healthcare and medicines policy: offering prescription drugs without a prescription is not allowed and is treated as egregious. Prescription drug terms are restricted in ads, landing pages and keywords. "Promotion of speculative and/or experimental medical treatments" is not allowed. Certification is required for prescription drug services, pharma manufacturers, addiction services and health insurance. India-specific rules: online pharmacies are allowed only with LegitScript and Google certification and may not promote prescription drugs. OTC pharma manufacturers need certification. Prenatal gender determination ads are banned in India. — [Google Ads Healthcare and medicines policy](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/176031)

### Inferences
- For a pulmonologist's GBP: fill in factual fields (name as "Dr. X, MD (Pulmonary Medicine)" only if this matches Google's naming rules, category, address, hours, phone, services list, website link). Do not post "promotional" GBP updates with offers. Do not send review-request links or campaigns. Responding to reviews should be non-promotional and must never confirm that the reviewer is a patient or disclose clinical details (IMC 7.14 confidentiality; DPDP Act).
- Running Google Ads for the clinic is not forbidden by Google for general consultation services. Under NMC 3.2/3.5, "search-engine" and "sponsored" promotion is advertising, and 8.1(ii) bars self-promotion. Paid search ads for an individual RMP are therefore high-risk under NMC rules regardless of Google approval. Landing pages must also avoid prescription drug names.
- Telemedicine landing pages that mention prescribing could fall under Google's prescription-drug-services restrictions.

### Gaps
- Google's separate GBP rules for "Healthcare providers" (e.g. individual practitioner listings vs practice listings, naming conventions) were not fetched in this pass.
- Whether Google Ads currently requires any India-specific healthcare-provider verification for clinics was not confirmed. The policy page showed none for general medical services.

## Consumer-protection angle: Consumer Protection Act 2019 / CCPA guidelines and the Drugs and Magic Remedies Act

### Takeaway
Doctors' medical services fall within consumer law, and misleading health claims can attract action from the CCPA (Central Consumer Protection Authority) under the 2019 Act. The CCPA's 2022 misleading-advertisement guidelines and the Aug 2023 health and wellness endorsement guidelines require certified medical practitioners to disclose their credentials when making health claims and to avoid unsubstantiated claims. The **Drugs and Magic Remedies (Objectionable Advertisements) Act, 1954 is a criminal statute**. It bars taking part in any advertisement suggesting a drug for diagnosing, curing or treating scheduled diseases, which include **Tuberculosis, Pneumonia and Pleurisy**. All are core pulmonology conditions, so drug- or remedy-cure claims for these on the site are especially dangerous.

### Cited Findings
- The Department of Consumer Affairs issued "Additional guidelines" on 10 Aug 2023 for health and wellness celebrities, influencers and virtual influencers. Certified medical practitioners and health and fitness experts must disclose their certified status when sharing information, promoting health products or making health-related claims. Endorsers must urge audiences to consult healthcare professionals and avoid specific health claims without credible evidence. The guidelines extend the 2022 Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements. General health advice not linked to specific products or services is excluded. — [Medical Dialogues](https://medicaldialogues.in/news/health/govt-mandates-disclosure-or-disclaimer-for-celebrities-influencers-endorsing-as-health-experts-115852); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/healthcare/1359032/additional-guidelines-for-health-wellness-celebrities-and-influencers); [Drishti IAS](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/additional-guidelines-for-health-and-wellness-celebrities/print_manually)
- DMR Act s.2(a): "advertisement" includes "any notice, circular; label, wrapper, or other document, and any announcement made orally or by any means of producing or transmitting light, sound or smoke". s.3: "no person shall take any part in the publication of any advertisement referring to any drug in terms, which suggest or are calculated to lead to the use of, that drug for … (d) The diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of any disease, disorder or condition specified in the Schedule". s.7: up to 6 months' imprisonment and/or fine for a first conviction, and up to 1 year for subsequent convictions. — [Legal Authority bare act](https://www.legalauthority.in/bare-act/drugs-and-magic-remedies-1954)
- The DMR Schedule (54 items) includes **Pleurisy (41), Pneumonia (42), Tuberculosis (50)**, plus Cancer, Heart diseases, High or low blood pressure, Diabetes, Obesity and others. Asthma and bronchitis do not appear. — [Legal Authority bare act](https://www.legalauthority.in/bare-act/drugs-and-magic-remedies-1954)
- The Supreme Court has read the DMR Act as covering objectionable advertisements broadly, not only those for scheduled diseases. A 2020 draft amendment proposed an expanded schedule, but its enactment was not confirmed. — [iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/pharmaceutical-advertising-in-india-prohibited-or-regulated-under-the-drug-laws-of-india/)
- The NMC Guidelines' 8.1(iv) separately bars an RMP from endorsing any drug, device or health product. — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- LexCounsel notes the Guidelines' practical impact will depend on how they interact with "other applicable advertising and consumer-protection legislation". — [Medical Dialogues / LexCounsel](https://medicaldialogues.in/articles/nmc-introduces-new-ethical-rules-for-healthcare-advertising-compliance-and-enforcement-challenges-for-hospitals-in-india-181825)

### Inferences
- Condition pages (TB, pneumonia, COPD, asthma, ILD, sleep apnoea) should be educational: symptoms, when to see a doctor, how diagnosis is made, general treatment approaches. They should not name branded drugs or suggest that any drug "cures" a condition, and should include a "consult a doctor; not a substitute for medical advice" disclaimer. "TB is curable with a full course of treatment under the National TB Elimination Programme" is public-health information and likely acceptable. "We cure TB in X weeks" is not.
- Misleading-claim exposure under the CPA/CCPA is low for a factual site but real for claims such as "guaranteed", "permanent cure" or "100% results".

### Gaps
- The text of the CCPA Guidelines for Prevention of Misleading Advertisements and Endorsements, 2022 (e.g. definitions of "bait advertisement", "surrogate advertisement", disclaimers) was not fetched directly.
- Whether the DMR Act rules add diseases beyond the Schedule, and the status of the 2020 amendment draft, were not confirmed.
- No CCPA enforcement case against an individual doctor's website was found.

## Practical guidance: safe vs risky wording and features for a pulmonologist's website and GBP

### Takeaway
Build the site as a factual patient-information resource. Treat anything that praises, ranks, counts, compares, quotes patients, offers deals or shows outcomes as prohibited. Mandatory items are name, recognised qualifications and SMC/NMR registration number. Fee display is permitted if factual.

### Cited Findings
- Required or safe items are supported by: 2002 Reg 1.4.1 (registration number displayed in clinic, prescriptions, certificates and receipts), 1.4.2 (only recognised degrees as suffixes), 7.13 (signboard content: name, qualifications, specialty, registration number with SMC name, hours), 1.8 (announce fees before service). — [bnblegal bare act](https://bnblegal.com/bareact/the-indian-medical-council-professional-conduct-etiquette-and-ethics-regulations-2002/). Also 2026 Guidelines 3.2 Expl. III (name, qualifications, registration status and SMR/NMR number on electronic-media posts), 8.2/9.3 (announcements of starting practice, address, absence, fees) and 9.1 (directory-style profile = patient information). — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- Prohibited items are supported by 2026 Guidelines 3.2 Expl. V, 6.2, 7.2, 8.1(i)–(xii) and 9.2 (see section 2). — [NMC notice PDF](https://medicaldialogues.in/pdf_upload/2026/10/07/nmc-guidelines-on-ethical-advertising-and-public-communication-478642.pdf)
- LexCounsel's recommended compliance steps: review websites, social media, videos, sponsored content and agency arrangements; set up internal approval for public content; scrutinise outcome claims, testimonials, discounts, referral arrangements and AI content; stick to factual, verifiable information. — [Medical Dialogues / LexCounsel](https://medicaldialogues.in/articles/nmc-introduces-new-ethical-rules-for-healthcare-advertising-compliance-and-enforcement-challenges-for-hospitals-in-india-181825)

### Inferences
**Safe / recommended (derived from the cited rules)**
- Header or footer on every page: "Dr. [Full Name], MBBS, MD (Pulmonary Medicine) [exact recognised degrees only], Consultant Pulmonologist, Telangana Medical Council Reg. No. [xxxxx]" (plus NMR ID if the doctor has one).
- Clinic name, address, map, phone or WhatsApp number for appointments, consulting hours, and a holiday or absence notice.
- Neutral service list: "Consultation for cough, breathlessness, asthma, COPD, TB, ILD, sleep-disordered breathing; Pulmonary function test (spirometry); …". No adjectives such as "advanced" or "state-of-the-art" used comparatively.
- Consultation fee stated plainly ("Consultation fee: ₹___; Spirometry: ₹___"), with no "discount" or "free first visit".
- Factual career history: medical college, residency, previous posts, memberships (with recognised bodies), and publications in peer-reviewed journals (8.2(i)).
- Educational condition pages and patient leaflets with references and a disclaimer. No drug brand names. No cure guarantees.
- An optional plain link "View on Google Maps / Google Business Profile" with no star widgets, no "see our 5-star reviews", no embedded review carousel. This is lowest risk but untested; the strictest option is to omit it.
- A privacy notice and consent text for appointment forms (DPDP Act 2023/Rules 2025). Collect minimal health data via the website.
- An AI-content label on any AI-generated illustration.

**Risky / avoid**
- "Best / top / leading / No.1 / most trusted pulmonologist in Hyderabad", including in page titles, meta descriptions, GBP description and SEO keywords. Search-engine content counts as advertising (3.2), and SEO stuffing with superlatives is a visible violation.
- Patient testimonials, video stories, embedded Google review widgets, "What our patients say" sections, and reposting reviews on Instagram, WhatsApp or Facebook.
- "5000+ patients treated", "98% success rate", "1000 bronchoscopies done", celebrity patients, before/after X-ray or CT images used promotionally, and patient case stories (even with consent).
- Offers: free camps that function as solicitation, discount coupons, "first consultation free", referral rewards, pay-per-lead agencies.
- Asking patients for Google reviews (QR codes at reception, SMS or WhatsApp review requests). Google permits genuine-review requests but NMC Expl. V and 8.1(ii) make this risky. Never use incentives or gating, which violate both.
- Promotional use of the doctor's photo (large hero image, ad banners). A single small identification headshot is a grey zone.
- Paid Google or Meta ads promoting the individual doctor.
- Endorsing any inhaler or device brand, or listing brand names with claims.
- AI-generated "doctor videos" or synthetic patient images.
- Fear-based copy ("Your cough could be cancer – book now!").

### Gaps
- No official SMC or NMC model template for a compliant doctor website exists in the sources found. The safe/risky lists above are inferences from the rule text, not regulator-approved wording.
- How strictly TGMC will interpret "self-promotion" for a basic personal website, as opposed to an aggressive marketing site, is untested in reported cases.
